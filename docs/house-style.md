# House style

What the rewriting pass is for, and what it is not allowed to do. It is short
on purpose: the worked examples below carry more of it than the rules do, and
the rules that matter are the ones about not inventing things.

## The voice

Write for someone who is interested and not obliged — who came to read about
Babylon because Babylon is interesting, and who will leave if the first
sentence is a definition.

That is not the same as writing *breezily*. The subject is three thousand years
of somebody's life and the evidence is thin and argued-over; a tour guide's
enthusiasm patronises both. The register to aim at is a good popular historian
writing for a general audience: plain, specific, confident about what is known,
candid about what is not.

**Concrete before abstract.** A number, a name, a thing you could photograph.
**Say what is at stake.** Why does it matter that the Gutians ruled for a
century? **Keep the uncertainty.** "Probably", "no contemporary source says",
"the only account is four hundred years later" — these are the content, not
hedging to be tidied away.

## Worked examples

Real leads from the corpus. The *after* invents nothing: every fact in it is in
the *before* or in the same document's front matter.

**Hanging Gardens of Babylon — before**

> The Hanging Gardens of Babylon were one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient
> World listed by Hellenic culture. They were described as a remarkable feat of
> engineering with an ascending series of tiered gardens containing a wide
> variety of trees, shrubs, and vines, resembling a large green mountain
> constructed of mud bricks.

**after**

> The Hanging Gardens are the only one of the Seven Wonders whose location is
> unknown — and the only one that may never have existed. Greek and Roman
> writers described tiers of trees, shrubs and vines rising out of Babylon's
> mud brick like a green mountain, watered somehow at the top. No Babylonian
> text mentions them.

What changed: the lead now opens on the thing that makes the subject worth
reading about, which was in the article all along, four sections down. Nothing
was added. "Listed by Hellenic culture" became the Greek and Roman writers who
did the listing, because that is who they were.

**Gutian rule in Mesopotamia — before**

> The Gutian dynasty was a dynasty, originating among the Gutian people, that
> came to power in Mesopotamia c. 2199 BC or c. 2135 BC, and which ended with
> the rise of the Third Dynasty of Ur.

**after**

> For about a century after the Akkadian empire fell, Mesopotamia was ruled by
> people its own scribes could barely describe. The Gutians came from the
> Zagros mountains; the cities they took wrote them down as a catastrophe, and
> then wrote almost nothing else. Even the date they arrived is argued over by
> sixty years.

What changed: "was a dynasty that was a dynasty" is a definition eating its own
tail. The dates that were a parenthetical are the point — a sixty-year
disagreement about when a century began is worth a sentence of its own.

## The ban list

The failure mode of "make it engaging" is a voice worse than the dry original.
These are the specific ways it goes wrong.

- **No second-person time travel.** "Imagine standing in the shadow of the
  ziggurat." "Picture yourself walking the Processional Way."
- **No rhetorical questions as openers.** "But who were the Gutians really?"
- **No dramatic irony.** "Little did they know." "The empire would not last."
- **No mystery-mongering.** "Historians remain baffled." They are usually not
  baffled; they disagree, and saying which way is more interesting anyway.
- **No superlative inflation.** Not every city is "one of the greatest of the
  ancient world". If the article does not say it, it is not true here.
- **No throat-clearing.** "It is important to note that." "Perhaps the most
  fascinating aspect of."
- **No modern analogies the source does not make.** Babylon is not "the New
  York of the ancient world" unless somebody cited said so.
- **No em-dash-and-clause rhythm on every sentence.** Vary the length.

## The rules that are not style

These are conditions of the work rather than preferences about it.

1. **Closed book.** Use only what is in the document you are given: its front
   matter, its body, its Wikidata claims. Not what you know about Babylon. If
   the document does not contain it, it does not go in — and this is the rule
   that makes everything else checkable, because every sentence has a source in
   the same file you were handed.
2. **Citations ride along.** A rewritten sentence carries the `[[ ^12 ]]` of the
   claim it came from. Where two sentences merge, both references come with it.
   A sentence that cannot keep its citation does not get written.
3. **Copy the hard values.** Dates, numbers, names, titles, measurements and
   quotations are transcribed exactly, not paraphrased. `c. 2200 BC` stays
   `c. 2200 BC`; it does not become "around 2000 BC".
4. **Keep the headings.** Section headings are reproduced verbatim. They are
   what the outline, the contents rail and every incoming link are built from,
   and a rewrite is of the prose, not of the structure.
5. **Keep the hedges.** If the source says "probably", "may have", "according to
   Herodotus", so does the rewrite. Removing a hedge is inventing a fact.
6. **Do not add sections, and do not drop one.** Every heading in, every heading
   out.
